Research Associate & PhD Candidate | Safety, Cybersecurity & AI for Automated Driving
Prodigy in an infant state on career progression path.

A vehicle that drives itself has to recognise people, other vehicles and the road. It does this with AI models trained on recorded data. Nobody writes down in advance how such a model must behave, so engineers cannot check it line by line against a specification. The standards for vehicle safety assume exactly that kind of check. My research works out how to show that an AI-based perception system is safe and secure enough to be released, and that it stays so on the road.

I work at the Institute for Driver Assistance and Connected Mobility (IFM) of Kempten University of Applied Sciences in Germany, as a Research Associate since 2021 and as a PhD candidate. I came to this field through control engineering: a degree in Instrumentation and Control Engineering in India, a first job building a monitoring system for quality checks in the machine tool industry, and a Master’s in Smart Systems in Germany. My master’s thesis on testing automated lane-keeping in simulation brought me to Kempten.

Research

I test AI-based perception in simulation, under conditions that are rare or dangerous to recreate on a real road, and I turn the results into evidence that a safety assessor can check. The work follows a vehicle’s AI from development to operation.

Before release

Does the AI still detect pedestrians in rain, fog or darkness?

I build simulated sensor data for such conditions and measure how uncertain the AI is about each detection. Where several AI models disagree, the detection is likely wrong.

VEHITS 2024 and 2026, Springer 2026

At release

Can one argument show that the system is both safe and secure?

Heavy rain and a sensor attack can hide the same pedestrian. Safety standards cover the rain and security standards cover the attack, but neither assigns the failure that both can cause. I build one argument that covers both and shows where the gaps remain.

On the road, the vehicle sees a sudden drop in detection quality but not its cause. Runtime monitoring, as in the animation above, is one part of the answer.

The questions sit between four ISO standards: ISO 26262 for faults in hardware and software, ISO 21448 for hazards that arise without any fault (also called SOTIF), ISO/SAE 21434 for cybersecurity, and ISO/PAS 8800 for AI in road vehicles.
